About the concept

Zoom in far enough on a smooth surface and it looks flat. That flat piece is the tangent plane: the best linear approximation of \(z=f(x,y)\) near the point \(P_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\), where \(z_0=f(x_0,y_0)\).

1Write the graph as a level surface

\[F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)-z,\qquad \text{surface: } F(x,y,z)=0\]

2The gradient is normal to a level surface

Along any curve \(\mathbf r(t)\) on the surface, \(F(\mathbf r(t))=0\), so \(\tfrac{d}{dt}F=\nabla F\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=0\). The gradient is perpendicular to every direction you can move in on the surface.

\[\nabla F(P_0)=\big(f_x(x_0,y_0),\ f_y(x_0,y_0),\ -1\big)\]

\(\nabla F\) points down into the surface, so the black arrow shows \(-\nabla F=(-f_x,-f_y,1)\). It is just as normal and points up.

3In the plane means perpendicular to the normal

A point \(P=(x,y,z)\) is in the tangent plane exactly when the vector from \(P_0\) to \(P\) is perpendicular to the normal:

\[\nabla F(P_0)\cdot(P-P_0)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f_x(x-x_0)+f_y(y-y_0)-(z-z_0)=0\]

4Solve for \(z\)

\[z=z_0+f_x(x_0,y_0)\,(x-x_0)+f_y(x_0,y_0)\,(y-y_0)\]

5Check it with the two traces

Cut the surface with \(y=y_0\): the curve has slope \(f_x\), so \(\mathbf u=(1,0,f_x)\) is tangent to it. Cut with \(x=x_0\): \(\mathbf v=(0,1,f_y)\). Both lie in the plane, so their cross product is the same normal.

\[\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\1&0&f_x\\0&1&f_y\end{vmatrix}=(-f_x,\,-f_y,\,1)=-\nabla F(P_0)\]

Why it matters: The tangent plane is the 3D version of the tangent line. It gives linear approximations and differentials \(dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy\), and it is flat (\(f_x=f_y=0\)) at every maximum, minimum and saddle point — try the bowl, the bump and the saddle at the origin.
